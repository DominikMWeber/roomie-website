Let me introduce roomie: an old-school, nerdy and customizable intercom system that lets roommates talk to each other from different locations at home — running on ESP32 platforms.

As a godfather raising a 4-year-old lad, I wanted to provide a system that introduces kids to communication technology through real conversations, in a setup that's fun to use. A setup that reduces screen time away from feature-overloaded, addictive smartphones.

Roomie is designed to be a simple and responsive counterpart to the modern world's smart devices, with their tiny, non-intuitive controls without haptic feedback and an overwhelming number of device states.

The design language and user interface are inspired by video game consoles from the 90s and 2000s: two arcade buttons that provide immediate, satisfying feedback and a Game Boy-style volume control slider.

The usability is simple: hold the green button to initiate a broadcast call or to accept a call — press it briefly to send a non-interruptible audio notification. Press the red button to reject a call or hang up. Custom call sounds for playback can be provided via SD card.

Developing roomie was also intended to be a sustainable learning experience for myself, rather than a quick-outcome vibe-coding experience. I studied many books and courses in digital signal processing, building professional embedded systems, debugging techniques and design thinking.

The method that probably influenced roomie's design the most is the concept of "putting information in the world" from Don Norman's book *The Design of Everyday Things*: make devices discoverable and so easy to understand that they become self-explanatory. This is the major mantra of human-centered design — making machines align to human behavior, rather than making humans satisfy the machine's needs.

With this website, I want to reach all of you: This website is intended to be a resource for tech enthusiasts who want to build roomie together with their family, kids, roommates, or partner and use it at home. The website’s playful design shouldn’t obscure the fact that roomie is the result of semi-professional development work. For the future, I plan to use this project to offer a free self-study course on this website that provides an introduction to professional firmware development at technical college level.

Many thanks to my friend Isabell Waghals for designing the PCB layout.
